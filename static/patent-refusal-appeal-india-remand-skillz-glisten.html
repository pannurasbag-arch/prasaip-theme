Appealing a patent refusal in India: three 2026 rulings

Two High Court appeals sent refused applications back to the Patent Office. In one, claims were cut on appeal. In the other, the refusal rested on a ground never put to the applicant. A third upheld a refusal on inventive step. We explain what each means for applicants.

By Praneetha K RasbagPublished 23 November 20267 minute read

Can a patent refused in India be revived on appeal? Yes, and recent High Court rulings show two common routes. An applicant that narrows its claims on appeal may obtain a remand for fresh examination. A refusal based on an objection that was not in the hearing notice may be set aside for breach of natural justice. Where neither applies, the court tests the Controller's reasoning on the merits and may uphold the refusal, as in TVS.

This note covers three appeals under section 117A of the Patents Act, 1970. The first is Glisten LLC v Assistant Controller (Delhi High Court, 1 October 2026). The Madras High Court decided the other two. These are Skillz Platform Inc v Assistant Controller (11 November 2026) and TVS Motor Company v Deputy Controller (12 November 2026).

Key points
  • In Skillz, the applicant dropped its method claim on appeal and kept the system claim. The court remitted the application to a different Controller, limited to the system claim, with a four month deadline.
  • In Glisten, the refusal rested on obviousness of claims 1 to 17, but the hearing notice had raised inventive step only against claim 18. The refusal was set aside and the application revived.
  • Glisten holds that the hearing notice under section 14 tells the applicant what still stands against the application. A new or revived objection must be put to the applicant, with a real chance to answer, before refusal.
  • In TVS, the court upheld a refusal. Moving a known biometric device to the fuel tank of a vehicle was obvious over the two cited documents.
  • None of these orders grants a patent. Remand only returns the application for a fresh decision.

Glisten: a ground missing from the hearing notice

Glisten applied for a patent on a gemstone coating. The First Examination Report objected that claims 1 to 18 lacked inventive step over documents D1 and D2. Glisten replied. The hearing notice of 14 June 2024 then carried forward an inventive step objection only against claim 18, based on lack of working examples. Glisten gave up claim 18. The Controller refused the application anyway, holding claims 1 to 17 obvious over D1 and D2.

Justice Purushaindra Kumar Kaurav set the refusal aside. The court described the hearing notice as "the Controller's statement of what, after considering the reply, still stands against the application" (paragraph 28). An applicant may prepare for the hearing on the objections listed in it. If the Controller wants to revive or add an objection, the applicant must be told and given a real opportunity to answer.

The court also found clear prejudice. The order rejected Glisten's wear test data for lack of an unexpected technical effect, a point never raised with the applicant (paragraph 32). General written submissions after the hearing could not replace notice. The application was restored for fresh consideration, with all rights left open on the merits.

Skillz: claims narrowed on appeal

Skillz's application was refused, with the Controller relying on sections 3(k) and 3(m). On appeal, Skillz sought to omit the method claim and pursue only the system claim. The Patent Office argued that its order was detailed and dealt with every claim, so no remand was needed.

Justice P.B. Balaji followed the Madras High Court's earlier decisions in Optimedica Corporation v Assistant Controller and TVS Motor Company v Controller (2024). He held that even omitting a claim "will certainly have an impact on the final conclusion" of the Controller (paragraph 5). The application was remitted to a different Controller, limited to the system claim, to be decided within four months.

If needed, the Controller may seek an opinion from a scientific adviser under section 115, such as a professor at IIT Madras. The Patent Office will bear the cost. The order does not decide whether the system claim falls within section 3(k). That question returns to the Patent Office. TVS: a refusal upheld on the merits

TVS Motor sought a patent for a vehicle with a biometric rider recognition system on the fuel tank. The Controller found no inventive step over D1 and D2. Those documents disclosed the same system near the handlebar and in the glove box.

Justice K. Kumaresh Babu dismissed the appeal on 12 November 2026. The court accepted that technical advantages can be considered. But placing the identification device on a different surface of the vehicle could not make the claim a new invention (paragraph 16). Having upheld the obviousness finding, the court did not decide the other grounds.

Applicants relying on a new position for a known component should expect to prove a technical effect beyond the position itself. Our note on inventive step and mosaicing covers how Indian courts combine prior art.

What the rulings do not decide

  • No patent was granted. Skillz and Glisten return to the Patent Office for a fresh decision on the merits.
  • Skillz does not create a general right to amend claims on appeal. It applies earlier Madras High Court decisions where claims were amended pending the appeal.
  • Glisten does not stop a Controller from refusing on inventive step. It requires that the objection be put to the applicant first.
  • TVS does not hold that every relocation of a component is obvious. It turned on two documents that disclosed the same system in other positions.

How the US and Europe compare

United States. An applicant whose claims have been twice rejected may appeal to the Patent Trial and Appeal Board under 35 U.S.C. § 134(a). A further appeal lies to the Federal Circuit, or a civil action may be brought in district court. A new ground of rejection in the examiner's answer must be designated as such under 37 C.F.R. § 41.39(a)(2). Within two months the applicant must either request reopening of prosecution or maintain the appeal with a reply brief, under § 41.39(b). Amendments after an appeal is filed are restricted under 37 C.F.R. § 41.33.

European Patent Office. Article 113(1) EPC provides that decisions may only be based on grounds or evidence on which the parties have had an opportunity to comment. This is comparable to the Glisten principle. An appeal goes to a Technical Board of Appeal, which may decide the case or remit it under Article 111(1). The Rules of Procedure of the Boards of Appeal limit amendments filed late in the appeal, so claim requests should be filed early.

Practical steps

  • Applicants should compare the hearing notice line by line with the First Examination Report. Note which objections were dropped and keep the hearing submissions focused on what remains.
  • Applicants refused on a ground not in the hearing notice should consider an appeal on natural justice, citing Glisten and the cases it follows.
  • Applicants appealing a section 3(k) refusal should consider whether a narrower set of claims, such as system claims alone, would stand better on remand.
  • Applicants relying on a new position or arrangement of known parts should file evidence of the technical effect during prosecution, not only on appeal.
  • All applicants should track deadlines closely. Our note on abandonment after a missed FER deadline shows how a lapse can end an application before any appeal.

Frequently asked questions

Can a refused patent application be appealed in India?

Yes. An appeal against the Controller's refusal lies to the High Court under section 117A of the Patents Act, 1970. The court may set the order aside, remand the application or uphold the refusal.

Can the Controller refuse a patent on a ground not raised in the hearing notice?

Not without first putting it to the applicant, according to the Delhi High Court in Glisten (1 October 2026). The applicant must be told and given a real opportunity to answer before an adverse order is passed.

Can I amend my claims during a patent appeal in India?

The Madras High Court has remanded applications where claims were amended on appeal, in Optimedica, TVS (2024) and Skillz. The Patent Office then examines the amended claims afresh.
