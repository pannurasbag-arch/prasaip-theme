Startup IP on a budget. What to patent first and what can wait.

A large early portfolio can drain a young company through fees in every country, every year. Protect what the business actually needs, in the order it needs it.

Published 29 September 20269 minute read

A large patent and trade mark portfolio can look impressive in a pitch deck. For an early startup it can also become a recurring bill that grows every year, in every country, whether or not the product survives.

Patents and trade marks are not one time costs. Each patent family carries drafting and filing fees, examination fees, attorney fees for every office action, and renewal fees that usually rise with age. Each country multiplies the bill. A startup that files widely in its first year can find that its IP budget competes with hiring and product work just when cash matters most. The answer is not to avoid IP. It is to file for what the business actually needs, in the order it needs it.

Start with the business question, not the filing question

Before any application, ask what the company must stop competitors from doing in the next three to five years. The answer is usually narrower than the list of everything the engineers have built. A patent is worth its cost when it protects the feature that customers pay for, when copying would hurt the business, and when the company would realistically enforce it or license it.

Five questions before filing a patent
  • Is the invention central to how the company earns revenue or raises money?
  • Could you detect a competitor using it? Features inside a product are easier to police than server side processes.
  • Will it still matter when the patent is granted, which often takes several years?
  • Would you enforce it, license it or rely on it in a sale of the company?
  • Could secrecy protect it better than publication in a patent?

If most answers are no, a patent is probably not the right spend yet. Good records, confidentiality and a clear ownership chain may protect the company better for a fraction of the cost.

Protect the brand first, but keep the filing narrow

A startup uses its name from the first day it trades. A clearance search before launch costs far less than a rebrand after an objection or a demand letter. File the trade mark in the classes that cover what the company sells now and what it will sell soon. Filing in many classes for products that may never exist adds fees and can invite oppositions without adding real protection.

The main markets let a startup file before sales begin, but in different ways. India accepts applications for marks proposed to be used. The United States accepts intent to use applications, although registration only issues after the owner proves use in commerce. The European Union trade mark covers all 27 member states in one application, and fees for additional classes are modest compared with the first class. The United Kingdom now needs its own filing.

For markets abroad, the Paris Convention gives six months from the first trade mark filing to claim priority elsewhere. Once the target markets are clear, one international application under the Madrid Protocol can extend a home mark to many members, including India, the United States, the European Union and the United Kingdom. Stage patent spending so each step buys a decision

Patent procedure lets a startup spend in stages and decide as it learns. The first step differs by country, but the logic is the same.

  • A low cost first filing. India and the United States both offer provisional applications that secure a filing date at a lower cost, with the full application due within 12 months. Europe has no provisional application. A European startup can instead make a first filing at the EPO, which aims to issue a search report and written opinion within about six months, or at a national office such as the UK IPO, whose search fees are low. Either way, the first filing must describe the invention fully, because features left out do not get its date.
  • Decide at month 12. File the complete application at home, and decide whether protection abroad is worth pursuing. A PCT application keeps foreign options open for up to 30 or 31 months from the priority date.
  • Enter only the markets that matter. At the national phase, choose countries where the company sells, manufactures or expects to be copied. Every country added means years of fees.

Know the disclosure rules before you pitch or launch

The major systems treat early disclosure very differently, and the strictest one sets the rule for a startup that wants protection in several markets.

  • United States. An inventor has one year from their own public disclosure to file, under 35 U.S.C. section 102(b)(1).
  • Europe. The EPC applies absolute novelty. Article 55 only excuses a disclosure within six months that resulted from evident abuse, or display at an officially recognised international exhibition. A demo day or product launch is not excused.
  • India. Section 31 of the Patents Act protects only narrow categories of disclosure, such as notified exhibitions and papers read before learned societies.

Japan, Korea, Canada and Australia also give inventors a twelve month grace period for their own disclosures. China excuses only narrow categories. The practical rule is simple. If Europe or India may matter, file before any public disclosure. Relying on the US grace period can leave the invention unpatentable in Europe, India and other countries that apply absolute novelty.

Use fast tracks only when speed pays

A quick grant helps when a startup needs an asset for a funding round, a licence or an early copier. The USPTO offers Track One prioritized examination, which aims for a final decision within about 12 months on average, with reduced fees for small and micro entities and limits of four independent and thirty total claims. The EPO offers accelerated prosecution under PACE on request without an official fee. India offers expedited examination for startups in Form 18A. Faster examination also means faster office actions, so the claims should be ready before speed is requested.

Use every fee concession you qualify for

India

Startups recognised by DPIIT, natural persons, small entities and educational institutions pay reduced official patent fees. The Startup India portal describes the startup patent rebate as 80 per cent and the trade mark rebate as 50 per cent compared with other companies. Startups can also request expedited examination of patent applications, which can shorten the path to grant considerably.

The Start-ups Intellectual Property Protection scheme, known as SIPP, reimbursed the professional fees of empanelled facilitators. Published commentary reports that it ended on 31 March 2026, with no extension announced by mid 2026. The official fee concessions are separate and continue. Check the Startup India portal before relying on SIPP support.

United States

The USPTO gives small entities a 60 per cent reduction and micro entities an 80 per cent reduction on many patent fees. Micro entity status has income and filing history limits, with a separate route linked to US universities. Eligibility must hold at every fee payment, so it should be checked and recorded carefully. A wrong claim to reduced fees can cause serious problems later.

Europe

Since 1 April 2024, the EPO has cut many fees by 30 per cent for micro enterprises, natural persons, non profit organisations, universities and public research organisations. It is available only if the applicant has filed fewer than five European applications in the five years before the application concerned. SMEs that are not micro enterprises do not qualify.

PCT

WIPO reduces certain PCT fees by 90 per cent for natural persons who are nationals and residents of listed countries, including India. The reduction does not apply to companies. Choosing the Indian Patent Office as the International Searching Authority also keeps the search fee low and supports a request for expedited examination in India.

Prune the portfolio every year

Renewal fees are where portfolios quietly drain cash. Indian renewal fees are payable every year and increase over the life of the patent. US maintenance fees fall due at three and a half, seven and a half and eleven and a half years after grant. A European patent carries annual fees at the EPO while pending and in each validated country after grant, except in the participating EU states when a Unitary Patent is chosen.

Review the portfolio at least once a year. Ask whether each right still covers the product, still matters in that country and still supports a licence, a sale or investor diligence. Letting a weak right lapse is a sound business decision, not a failure.

Low cost protection that every startup needs

  • Written IP assignment clauses for founders, employees and contractors. Investors check ownership before they check patents.
  • Confidentiality agreements before sharing technical detail with partners, vendors or potential investors.
  • Dated invention records, design files and code repositories that show who created what and when.
  • Trade secret discipline for algorithms, data and processes that customers never see, including access controls and exit procedures.
  • An open source policy, so that licence obligations do not undermine proprietary code.

Copyright in software code and content arises automatically in India, the United States and Europe. In the United States, registration is needed before suing over a US work, so early registration of core code or content can be worth its small fee. Trade secrets are protected by the Defend Trade Secrets Act in the United States, by the Trade Secrets Directive across the EU, and by contract and the law of confidence in India. Frequently asked questions

Do investors expect a startup to have patents?

Not always. Many investors look first for clean ownership of the technology and brand, a clearance search for the name, and a sensible filing plan. A few well chosen applications usually impress more than a large, unfocused portfolio.

Should a startup file a patent before launching the product?

Yes, especially if Europe or India may matter. The United States, Japan and Korea give inventors a one year grace period, but Europe applies absolute novelty and India excuses only narrow categories of disclosure.

How many trade mark classes should a startup file in?

File in the classes that cover the goods and services the company offers now or will offer soon. Add classes as the business expands.
