DUO versus DUO. When can two businesses use the same word?

An identical word is the starting point of a trade mark analysis, not its conclusion. Goods, customers, distinctiveness and the history of use decide the outcome.

Published 28 September 2026Updated 29 September 20266 minute read

Suppose a skincare brand and a furniture business both adopt DUO. Whether one can stop the other depends far less on the spelling than on the market in which each mark is used.

This is a hypothetical example. We have not identified a reported Indian judgment between parties described as DUO and DUO, and nothing here suggests that any real business has asserted, won or lost a claim. The analysis applies the Trade Marks Act, 1999 and settled Supreme Court authority to an illustrative set of facts.

Start with the goods, the services and the real customers

Section 11 of the Act sets out the relative grounds for refusing registration, including an earlier identical or similar mark for identical or similar goods where confusion is likely. Section 29 defines infringement of a registered mark and applies different tests depending on how closely the marks and the goods correspond. In each case the question is how the ordinary purchaser encounters the marks in trade.

Classification is only an administrative tool. Goods in different classes can still be commercially connected, and buyers may assume a common source. An identical word does not give one trader control of every market either. A mark with a reputation in India receives wider protection. Under section 11(2) and section 29(4), use of a similar mark for dissimilar goods may be restrained where it takes unfair advantage of, or is detrimental to, the distinctive character or repute of the earlier mark. Those provisions have their own conditions, and reputation must be proved.

Consider DUO skincare sold through beauty retailers and DUO sofas sold through furniture showrooms. Their purposes, trade channels and buyers differ, and the risk of confusion may be low. Now change the facts. The furniture business launches DUO home fragrances, sells them in the same lifestyle stores, uses a similar gold device of two circles and markets the range as a companion collection. The risk rises sharply, even though the registration classes may be unchanged. Search results, advertising, packaging, social media handles and actual customer enquiries often reveal more than the register does.

Ask how distinctive DUO is for the particular goods

Section 9 bars registration of marks that lack distinctive character or that describe the kind, quality or other characteristics of the goods. The proviso allows registration where a mark has acquired distinctiveness through use before the application date. The word duo ordinarily means a pair. For a product sold as a set of two, or a service offered to couples, that meaning makes exclusivity harder to claim than it would be for an invented word. For goods unconnected with pairs, the word may be arbitrary and inherently stronger.

Strength also grows with use. Sales figures, advertising expenditure, press coverage and evidence of customer association can show that the public links DUO with one source. The strength of a mark must be assessed for the goods in question and cannot be assumed from its spelling.

Earlier use can outweigh an earlier filing

Section 34 protects a person who has continuously used a mark, from a date earlier than both the registered proprietor’s first use and the registration, against interference by the registered proprietor. In Neon Laboratories Ltd v Medical Technologies Ltd (2015), the Supreme Court upheld protection for a prior user against a party whose application was filed earlier but whose use began later. The first genuine use in the market carried decisive weight.

Registration also does not extinguish passing off. Section 27(2) preserves the action. In S Syed Mohideen v P Sulochana Bai (2015), the Supreme Court held that the rights of a prior user in passing off are superior to those of a later registered proprietor. A later registration is therefore never a complete answer to a genuine prior user.

A reliable chronology is essential evidence. It should record the date of first commercial use, the goods, the territories, continuity of use, invoices, advertisements and any changes in presentation. Filing and registration dates remain important. The full commercial history usually decides a dispute between two established users.

Coexistence is possible, but it is not automatic

Section 12 permits the Registrar to register identical or similar marks for the same or similar goods where there has been honest concurrent use or other special circumstances exist. Conditions and limitations may be imposed. This is a statutory discretion. It is not a general right to share a mark.

Where both businesses have legitimate histories, a written coexistence agreement can reduce risk. It can define the goods each party may cover, the territories, the visual presentation, the terms for online advertising and keyword bidding, and a procedure for handling instances of confusion. Such an agreement binds only its parties. It should be tested against the rights of others and its likely effect on consumers.

Clearing a short word mark before launch
  • Search the Indian register for identical and phonetically similar marks in relevant and commercially adjacent classes.
  • Examine actual marketplace use, including unregistered users, domain names and social media accounts.
  • Assess how descriptive the word is for the intended goods.
  • Record why the intended presentation and trade channels are or are not likely to cause confusion.
  • Repeat the review before expanding into neighbouring product categories.

The right question to ask

The useful question is not whether anyone can own DUO. It is what rights exist in this market, for these goods, against this particular use, on this evidence. A distinctive logo may help separate two brands, but it rarely cures a conflict in the dominant word. The answer can also change as either business grows, so clearance should be revisited at each expansion.

Frequently asked questions

Does an identical word always mean infringement?

No. Infringement of a registered mark depends on the goods, the likelihood of confusion or, for marks with a reputation, unfair advantage or detriment. Identity of the word is only one factor.

Can an unregistered earlier user stop a registered later user?

An unregistered user cannot sue for statutory infringement. It may succeed in passing off, and section 34 protects qualifying continuous prior use against the registered proprietor.

Is this based on a real DUO case?

No. The facts are illustrative. No reported DUO versus DUO judgment is cited or implied.
