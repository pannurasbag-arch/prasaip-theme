PATENTS · TRADE MARKS · DESIGNS · COPYRIGHT

IP glossary for India. 63 patent, trade mark and design terms, explained.

Short, sourced definitions of the terms that come up when protecting an invention or a brand in India, with the US and European terms Indian applicants meet most often.

By Sanath V Madgula, reviewed by Praneetha K RasbagUpdated 28 November 2026

This glossary defines 63 intellectual property terms used in Indian practice, each in one or two sentences with its official source. It covers patents, trade marks, designs and copyright, and the US and European terms Indian applicants meet when they file abroad.

Each definition states the general rule. Deadlines, forms and fees change from time to time, so check the linked statute, rule or office page before relying on a date or a fee. Where we have a fuller guide, the entry links to it.

How to use this glossary
  • Terms are grouped by stage: patent basics, filing, examination and grant, international filing, trade marks, and other rights.
  • Every entry names its official source, usually the Patents Act, 1970, the Patents Rules, 2003 or the Trade Marks Act, 1999.
  • Section and rule numbers refer to the law as amended up to the Patents (Amendment) Rules, 2024.
  • Use the A to Z list below to jump straight to a term.

Patent basics

Patent

A patent is an exclusive right granted by the government for an invention, letting the owner stop others from making, using, selling or importing it in that country. In India it is granted under the Patents Act, 1970 after examination. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 48

Invention

Under section 2(1)(j) of the Patents Act, an invention is a new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application. All three conditions must be met. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 2

Novelty

An invention is new if it has not been anticipated by publication in any document or by use in India or elsewhere before the filing date of the application with complete specification. Section 2(1)(l) calls this a new invention. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 2

Inventive step

Inventive step means the invention involves a technical advance or economic significance, or both, and is not obvious to a person skilled in the art. It is defined in section 2(1)(ja). Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 2

Industrial application

An invention is capable of industrial application if it can be made or used in an industry. Section 2(1)(ac) of the Patents Act sets this test. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 2

Section 3 exclusions

Section 3 of the Patents Act lists subject matter that is not an invention at all, even if it is new. Examples include discoveries, methods of agriculture, methods of medical treatment and mere arrangements of known devices. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 3

Section 3(k)

Section 3(k) excludes a mathematical or business method, a computer programme per se and algorithms. Software related inventions can still be patented in India when the claims show a technical effect or technical contribution. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 3

Section 3(d)

Section 3(d) excludes a new form of a known substance unless it shows enhanced efficacy. It is most often raised against pharmaceutical and chemical inventions.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 3

CRI Guidelines

The Guidelines for Examination of Computer Related Inventions are published by the Indian Patent Office. They explain how examiners apply section 3(k) to software and AI inventions, and the current version was issued in 2025. Read the full guide.

Source: IP India, Patents Act and examination guidance

Patent agent

A patent agent is a person registered with the Indian Patent Office to file and prosecute patent applications for others. Only registered patent agents may practise as agents before the Controller, although section 132 lets an advocate appear and plead. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, sections 127 and 132

Controller

The Controller is the officer of the Indian Patent Office who examines applications, hears applicants and decides whether to grant a patent. Under section 73 the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks is the Controller. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 73

Filing in India

Provisional specification

A provisional specification describes the invention without final claims and secures a filing date in India. The complete specification must follow within 12 months, or the application is deemed abandoned under section 9(1). Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 9

Complete specification

A complete specification fully describes the invention, the best method of performing it, and discloses the best method of performing it known to the applicant, and ends with the claims. It is filed in Form 2 under section 10. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 10

Claims

Claims are the numbered statements at the end of a complete specification that define the legal scope of the patent. They must relate to a single invention, be clear and succinct, and be fairly based on the description. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 10

Priority date

The priority date is the date from which a claim is judged against earlier disclosures. Under section 11 it is usually the filing date of the earliest application that fairly supports the claim. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 11

Form 1

Form 1 is the application for grant of a patent in India. It names the applicant, the inventors and the type of application, and is filed with Form 2 and the official fee. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Rules, 2003

Form 2

Form 2 is the format for a provisional or complete specification in India. It carries the title, description, claims and abstract. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 10

Form 3 and section 8

Form 3 is the statement and undertaking under section 8 about corresponding applications filed abroad. Under the 2024 amendments an updated statement is due within three months of the First Examination Report. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 8

Form 5

Form 5 is the declaration as to inventorship. It names the true and first inventors and is filed with the complete specification. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 10

Form 26

Form 26 is the power of attorney that authorises a registered patent agent to act for the applicant before the Patent Office. No official fee is payable on it. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 127

Form 28 and reduced fees

Natural persons, startups, small entities and educational institutions pay the lower official fee in India. A startup or small entity claims the lower rate by filing Form 28 with supporting evidence. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Rules, 2003, First Schedule

Foreign filing permission

Section 39 bars a resident of India from applying for a patent abroad without written permission, sought on Form 25. Permission is not needed if an Indian application was filed at least six weeks earlier and no secrecy direction is in force. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 39

Publication

An Indian patent application is ordinarily published in the Patent Office Journal 18 months after the filing or priority date, whichever is earlier. An applicant can request early publication on Form 9. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 11A

Form 9

Form 9 is the request for early publication of an Indian patent application. After publication, provisional rights against infringers can arise from the date of publication. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 11A

Examination and grant

Form 18

Form 18 is the request for examination of an Indian patent application. For applications filed on or after 15 March 2024 it is due within 31 months from the priority date or the filing date, whichever is earlier. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Rules, 2003, rule 24B

Form 18A

Form 18A is the request for expedited examination. It is open only to specified categories, including startups, small entities and applicants who chose India as the International Searching Authority. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Rules, 2003, rule 24C

First Examination Report (FER)

The First Examination Report is the Controller's first statement of objections to an application. The applicant has six months from its issue to put the application in order, extendable once by three months on Form 4. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Rules, 2003, rule 24B(5) and (6)

Form 4

Form 4 is the request for extension of time in Indian patent proceedings. For the FER reply it allows one extension of three months if filed before the six months end. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Rules, 2003, rule 24B(6)

Deemed abandonment

An application is treated as abandoned under section 21(1) if the applicant does not meet all the requirements raised within the prescribed period. Missing the FER deadline is the most common cause. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 21

Hearing

If objections remain after the written reply, the Controller will generally offer a hearing before refusing an application. Hearings can be held by video conference, and written submissions follow within fifteen days. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 14

Pre grant opposition

Any person may oppose a published application before grant by a representation under section 25(1). Grounds include lack of novelty, obviousness, excluded subject matter and failure to disclose foreign filings. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 25

Post grant opposition

An interested person may oppose a granted patent within one year from the date the grant is published, under section 25(2). An Opposition Board then examines the notice. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 25

Patent term

An Indian patent lasts 20 years from the date of filing of the application under section 53. For a PCT national phase application the term runs from the international filing date. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 53

Renewal fees

Annual renewal fees keep an Indian patent in force and start in the third year from the date of the patent. Paying four or more years in advance online attracts a ten per cent reduction. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 53

Form 27 (statement of working)

Form 27 is the patentee's statement on working of the patent in India. It is filed once for every period of three financial years, within six months after the period ends. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 146

International filing

Paris Convention priority

Under the Paris Convention an applicant who files in one member country has 12 months to file in others and claim the first filing date. In India such filings are convention applications under section 135. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Act, 1970, section 135

Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)

The PCT lets an applicant file one international application that can later enter the national phase in more than 150 countries. It delays the main national costs without itself granting a patent. Read the full guide.

Source: WIPO, PCT

PCT national phase in India

A PCT application must enter the Indian national phase within 31 months from the priority date. Relief after a missed date is narrow, so the deadline should be treated as fixed. Read the full guide.

Source: Patents Rules, 2003

International Searching Authority

An International Searching Authority searches a PCT application and issues a search report and written opinion. The Indian Patent Office acts as one, and choosing it can make an applicant eligible for expedited examination in India. Read the full guide.

Source: WIPO, PCT

US provisional application

A US provisional application secures a filing date without formal claims. A non provisional application must be filed within 12 months to keep that date under 35 U.S.C. 119(e). Read the full guide.

Source: USPTO, provisional applications

US grace period

Under 35 U.S.C. 102(b)(1), the inventor's own disclosure made one year or less before the effective filing date is not prior art. India's grace period is much narrower and covers only the cases listed in sections 29 to 34. Read the full guide.

Source: USPTO, MPEP 2152

European patent application

A European patent application filed at the European Patent Office leads, after one examination, to a bundle of national patents in the states the applicant validates. It is governed by the European Patent Convention. Read the full guide.

Source: EPO

Unitary Patent

A Unitary Patent is a European patent given unitary effect in the participating EU states with one request and one renewal fee. It is enforced before the Unified Patent Court. Read the full guide.

Source: EPO, Unitary Patent

Trade marks

Trade mark

A trade mark is a sign, such as a word, logo, shape, colour combination or sound, that tells customers who stands behind goods or services. In India it is governed by the Trade Marks Act, 1999. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999

Form TM-A

Form TM-A is the single application form for registering a trade mark in India under the Trade Marks Rules, 2017. One application can cover several classes, with a fee for each class. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 18

Trade mark classes

Goods and services are grouped into 45 classes of the international Nice Classification, 34 for goods and 11 for services. The Registrar classifies them under section 7. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 7

Absolute grounds for refusal

Section 9 lets the Registrar refuse marks that lack distinctive character, merely describe the goods or services, or have become customary in the trade. Marks likely to hurt religious susceptibilities are also barred. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 9

Relative grounds for refusal

Section 11 lets the Registrar refuse a mark that conflicts with an earlier mark for identical or similar goods or services, or with a well known mark. These objections depend on earlier rights. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 11

Trade mark examination report

After filing, the Trade Marks Registry examines the application and issues an examination report listing any objections. The applicant replies in writing and may be heard before a decision. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 18

Advertisement

An accepted trade mark application is advertised in the Trade Marks Journal under section 20. Advertisement opens the window for third parties to oppose. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 20

Trade mark opposition

Any person may oppose an advertised application within three months of advertisement, extendable by up to one more month, under section 21. The applicant must file a counter statement within two months of receiving the notice. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 21

Trade mark registration term

A registered trade mark in India lasts ten years and can be renewed for further ten year periods without limit, under section 25. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 25

Well known trade mark

Under section 2(1)(zg), a well known mark is one so familiar to the relevant public that its use on other goods or services would suggest a connection with the owner. Such marks get protection across classes. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 2

Passing off

Passing off is the common law action that protects an unregistered mark. The owner must show goodwill, misrepresentation by the other party and resulting damage. Read the full guide.

Source: Trade Marks Act, 1999, section 27

Madrid Protocol

The Madrid Protocol lets a trade mark owner seek protection in many countries through one international application based on a home application or registration. India joined the system in 2013. Read the full guide.

Source: WIPO, Madrid System

Designs, copyright and other rights

Industrial design

An industrial design is the shape, configuration, pattern, ornament or composition of lines or colours applied to an article and judged by the eye. In India it is registered under the Designs Act, 2000. Read the full guide.

Source: Designs Act, 2000

Design registration term

A registered design in India is protected for 10 years and can be extended once by 5 years, giving 15 years in total. Read the full guide.

Source: Designs Act, 2000

Locarno Classification

The Locarno Classification is the international system for classifying industrial designs. An Indian design application must name the Locarno class and the article. Read the full guide.

Source: WIPO, Locarno Classification

Design piracy

Copying a registered design, or an obvious imitation of it, on an article of the same class is piracy under the Designs Act. Remedies include an injunction and damages. Read the full guide.

Source: Designs Act, 2000

Copyright

Copyright protects original literary, artistic, musical and dramatic works, films, sound recordings and software. In India it arises automatically under the Copyright Act, 1957, and for most works lasts for the author's life plus 60 years. Read the full guide.

Source: Copyright Office, India

Copyright registration

Registration with the Indian Copyright Office is optional. It gives a certificate that serves as useful evidence of ownership in a dispute. Read the full guide.

Source: Copyright Office, India

Geographical indication

A geographical indication identifies goods as coming from a place where their quality or reputation is linked to that origin, such as Darjeeling tea. Registration lasts ten years and is renewable. Read the full guide.

Source: IP India, Geographical Indications Act and Rules

Trade secret

A trade secret is confidential business information that gives a competitive edge because it is kept secret. India has no separate statute, so protection comes through contracts and the law of confidence. Read the full guide.

Source: PRASA IP guide to types of IP

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a provisional and a complete specification?

A provisional specification describes the invention and secures a filing date. A complete specification adds the full description and the claims, and must be filed within 12 months of the provisional under section 9(1) of the Patents Act.

How long does a patent, trade mark and design last in India?

A patent lasts 20 years from filing. A trade mark registration lasts ten years and can be renewed without limit. A registered design lasts 10 years and can be extended once by 5 years.

What is an FER in Indian patent law?

An FER is the First Examination Report, the Controller's first list of objections to a patent application. The applicant has six months from its issue to respond, extendable once by three months on Form 4.
